How to Build a Scientific Scaling Ladder

This post draws strictly on public literature and personal hands-on experience and contains no confidential information. In my previous post, Pretraining and scaling: a universal methodology, I discussed why pretraining and scaling form a general methodology across domains; here I walk through how to build a reliable LLM pretraining scaling ladder from scratch.

1. Basics and Scope

1.1 What Is a Scaling Ladder

A scaling ladder is a matrix of small-scale pretraining runs spanning multiple model sizes $N$ and training token budgets $D$. In industry pretraining teams and frontier labs, it is much more than a tool for picking hyperparameters; it is the core experimental framework across the entire research cycle, serving three main roles:

  1. Studying scaling rules for hyperparameters and model configurations: mapping out how LR, BSZ, aspect ratio, MoE sparsity ratio, data mixtures, and epoch counts transfer as $(N, D)$ grows.
  2. Acting as a gold-standard baseline (optimal baseline) for new techniques: it serves as a common ruler. Any new optimizer, architectural change, or data batch must show consistent gains on the ladder before merging into the mainline recipe for the next base model. Many academic papers overclaim gains by testing at a single small scale or against an undertuned baseline; evaluating across scales on a well-tuned ladder filters out ideas that do not hold up and shows whether a real gain stays flat, grows, or fades with scale.
  3. Forecasting the flagship run (hero run): once the architecture, hyperparameter rules, and data recipe are locked in, the fitted curves predict the loss trajectory, final loss, and even downstream benchmark scores before committing full-scale compute.

Making that ruler reliable takes more than running a few small models and fitting a curve. If small baseline runs are undertuned, parameter counts and FLOPs are tallied inconsistently, data repetition epochs are mismatched across scales, or the functional form is misspecified, both method comparisons and large-scale forecasts will be off. In the first Delphi (Marin, 2026) attempt (Figure 1), the fit looked clean across $3\times 10^{18}$–$3\times 10^{20}$ FLOPs, yet actual loss came in 2.5% higher than predicted at $10^{22}$ FLOPs and training diverged outright at $10^{23}$ FLOPs. Building a scientific scaling ladder therefore requires both careful small-scale experiments and curve fitting, and multi-tier holdout runs plus stress tests to check that extrapolation actually holds (§10).

1.2 Typical Regimes

This post covers three pretraining regimes. Sections 2–10 lay out the shared workflow, while MoE-specific and data-constrained questions are addressed in dedicated subsections along the way.

2. Objectives and Acceptance Criteria

2.1 Decision Objectives

Before running anything, clarify what decision the ladder is meant to inform. Most ladders target one of four goals:

  1. Forecasting a fixed recipe: given a preset hyperparameter scaling rule, predict its large-scale performance without tuning each grid point individually.
  2. Optimal compute allocation: find the $(N, D)$ pair that minimizes loss under a compute budget $C$.
  3. Recipe comparison: compare architectures, optimizers, or data mixtures to see which wins at scale.
  4. Hyperparameter prediction: fit how optimal $(\eta, B, \lambda)$ scale with $N$ and $D$.

Delphi (Marin, 2026) separates whether a recipe is competitive (achieves lower loss) from whether it is predictable (extrapolates cleanly from small to large runs); both properties need to be checked separately. Goals 2–4 require tuning every fitting point onto the Fully-Tuned Frontier (§6.1), because poorly tuned small models tilt the fitted power-law slope. Goal 1 simply runs the prescribed hyperparameter formula directly.

2.2 Evaluation Metrics and Setup

A few practical points matter when setting up evaluation:

  • Separate proxy metrics from end goals: lower training loss on small models does not always translate into better downstream benchmark scores at scale (Qwen3.8-Next, 2026 §1).
  • Signal at small scales: small models often score near chance on hard math and code benchmarks, whereas easy tasks saturate at large scales. When discrete accuracy shows no signal on small models, continuous metrics such as bits-per-byte (BPB) on gold answers provide much cleaner separation (OLMo 3, 2025).
  • Rank consistency across scales: when using small-model proxy metrics to screen candidates, verify that small-scale rankings actually match large-scale ordering (OLMo 3, 2025 §3.3, Appendix A.4).
  • Handling high-variance benchmarks: cover core target capabilities, and average over multiple samples or track noisy benchmarks separately (Phi-4, 2024 §5; OLMo 3, 2025 §3.3.3–3.3.4).
  • Fixed evaluation harness: keep prompt templates, decoding parameters, and scoring script versions identical across runs, and check for train-test contamination.

If the final model will undergo SFT or RL, looking only at pretraining metrics can miss failure modes that surface during post-training. Qwen3.8-Next, 2026 gives two examples: removing positional encodings (NoPE) and adding sparse-read residual branches barely affected pretraining loss, yet caused non-terminating repetition and post-training degradation, respectively. OLMo 3, 2025 §3.5.1 addresses this by running lightweight instruction tuning after cooldown on candidate checkpoints and checking task scores, EOS termination, and output length together.

2.3 Acceptance Criteria

Set acceptable extrapolation error bounds ahead of time based on the smallest meaningful gap between candidate recipes and run-to-run seed noise, rather than after looking at holdout numbers. Choshen et al., 2025 report that the smallest relative improvement typically used to justify an architectural or algorithmic change in the literature is around 4%, while random-seed variance alone can reach roughly 3.5%; Delphi’s 0.2%–0.5% prediction error in its specific setup should not be treated as a universal bar.

A fixed relative loss error also means different things at different scales. Under a power law $L(C)=E+A\,C^{-\gamma}$, a loss gap $\Delta L$ translates into a compute multiplier of:

\[\ln\frac{C_2}{C_1}\approx\frac{\Delta L}{\gamma\,(L-E)}\]

As models grow and $L$ approaches the irreducible loss $E$, the same $\Delta L$ corresponds to a much larger compute ratio. Measuring gains and errors via the compute multiplier (e.g., 15% compute saved) alongside absolute $\Delta L$ is more informative than relative loss error alone.

3. Parameter, Compute, and Loss Accounting

3.1 Parameter Counting

Fitting $L(N,D)$ and computing FLOPs $C$ use different definitions of $N$, so record both separately.

When fitting $L(N,D)$, set $N$ to the backbone parameter count $N_{\text{body}}$, excluding both input embeddings and the output head. Embedding and head layers scale as $O(Vd)$ whereas the backbone scales as $O(d^2 n)$; at small model sizes, vocabulary layers make up a large fraction of total parameters and skew the fitted scaling exponent across sizes. Ablations in Farseer (Li et al., 2025) Appendix G show noticeably larger extrapolation error at 25.1B when embeddings are included than when using $N_{\text{body}}$; Kaplan et al., 2020 likewise excluded embeddings.

When computing training FLOPs $C$, however, you must add the output head back in. The output head performs a $d\times V$ matrix multiply on every token; tying weights with the input embedding saves memory, not that matrix multiply. Input embedding itself is just a table lookup by token ID and incurs no matrix-multiply FLOPs, so it is excluded from both.

Watch parameter definitions when comparing coefficients or TPP across papers: Porian et al., 2024 Table 2 includes the output head in $N$ while excluding input embeddings, whereas Chinchilla’s 20 TPP rule counts all parameters including embeddings (Hoffmann et al., 2022 Appendix F).

For MoE runs, log the following at every grid point:

  • Total backbone parameters $N_{total}$ and active parameters per token $N_{active}$ (both excluding embeddings and head);
  • Total routed experts $E_{total}$, active experts per token $E_{active}$, and sparsity ratio $S=E_{total}/E_{active}$;
  • Number and size of shared experts, plus individual routed expert size (expert granularity);
  • Router and load-balancing settings (§4.4).

3.2 Compute

The common shorthand $C\approx6ND$ drops attention and output-head FLOPs. For standard MHA with a $4d$ FFN and full $L\times L$ attention matrices, forward-plus-backward FLOPs are:

\[C \approx \left(6 + \frac{L}{d}\right) N_{\text{body}}D + 6VdD\]

where $L$ is sequence length, $d$ is hidden dimension, $V$ is vocabulary size, and $N_{\text{body}}\approx12d^2n$ for $n$ layers. The $L/d$ term comes from attention and the last term from the output head. If the attention kernel skips the masked upper triangle, the attention term halves; SwiGLU, GQA, and MoE also change the exact coefficients, so compute $C$ from the actual architecture and kernels in use.

For MoE models, matrix-multiply FLOPs follow $N_{active}$ plus router overhead; expert-parallel (EP) all-to-all communication adds zero FLOPs, though it directly affects wall-clock training speed (§10.4).

3.3 Loss and Token Accounting

Keep loss and token accounting consistent across every run on the ladder:

Item Rule
Loss term Report main next-token cross-entropy only; log MoE aux loss, z-loss, MTP, and other auxiliary terms separately
Validation set Share the exact same eval set and domain weights across all candidates, while tracking core domains individually
Loss reduction Average consistently per valid target token (or per document) across all runs
Sequence packing Standardize BOS/EOS placement, document boundaries, packing, attention masks, and truncation
Token accounting Distinguish total processed tokens, loss-contributing tokens, unique tokens, and repetition epochs
Random seeds Log initialization, data sampling, and shuffle seeds separately
Cross-tokenizer comparison Convert to BPB (bits-per-byte) on identical raw text and compare at matched FLOPs over the same text span

When experiments change the training data mixture, training loss reflects the intrinsic entropy of each mixture (for example, higher code share naturally lowers training loss) and cannot be compared across runs; evaluate on a fixed validation set instead.

Also space evaluations at fixed fractions of total training steps $T$ (e.g., every 2% of training) rather than fixed step intervals (e.g., every 1,000 steps). Fixed step intervals give long runs far more evaluation points than short runs and overweight them when fitting full loss curves (§8.3).

4. Baseline Setup and Scaling Rules

4.1 Grouping Variables

Variables on a ladder fall into three groups:

Group Meaning Examples
Held fixed Kept identical across all runs Architecture family, dataset version, optimizer type, seq_len
Scaled by rule Varied deterministically with $N$ or $D$ LR (power law or $\mu$P), BSZ (e.g., $D^{0.4}$), warmup fraction
Swept / fitted Independent variables to search or fit $N$, $D$, and the optimal LR and BSZ being swept

Set warmup steps as a fixed fraction of total training tokens (for example, Delphi uses a 10% warmup across all scales; see Appendix A.3). Keeping a constant warmup step count across scales makes warmup dominate short, small-budget runs and biases the fitted compute-optimal exponents (Porian et al., 2024).

Whenever the architecture, optimizer, dataset version, numerical precision, or tokenizer changes, existing scaling coefficients may stop holding; check on small runs first before deciding whether to reuse, adjust, or refit them (§7.1).

4.2 Architectural and Training Consistency

Aside from the specific variable under study, keep architectural and training choices aligned across all ladder sizes:

Component Guideline
Backbone Keep the same backbone family (e.g., decoder-only Transformer), normalization (e.g., Pre-RMSNorm), positional encoding (e.g., RoPE), and activation (e.g., SwiGLU)
Embeddings Keep embeddings either tied everywhere or untied everywhere
Attention Use the same attention variant (e.g., GQA), keeping KV group ratios and head_dim on small models close to the target model
Aspect ratio Scale width and depth together at a consistent ratio (§4.3)
MoE routing Use the same router, load-balancing method, and shared-expert ratio (§4.4)
Training and eval Match seq_len (e.g., 4096), data version, tokenizer, initialization, optimizer (e.g., AdamW), and validation setup (§3.3)

4.3 Aspect Ratio

At fixed backbone size $N_{\text{body}}$, changing the ratio of hidden width $d$ to layer count $n$ alters the attention compute term $L/d$ (§3.2). Kaplan et al., 2020 found pretraining loss largely insensitive to aspect ratio over a wide range, but later work shows aspect ratio affects downstream benchmarks (Gemstones, 2025 §4.4) and reasoning performance (GLM-4.5, 2025). Keep a consistent width-depth scaling rule across the ladder; if you want to compare different depths or head dimensions, running dedicated ablations is recommended.

4.4 MoE Structural Scaling

Beyond the general settings in §4.2, MoE ladders introduce several structural parameters:

  • Expert granularity: Krajewski et al., 2024 study individual expert size as an independent scaling variable and show that matching the dense FFN dimension is generally suboptimal; finer-grained experts widen MoE’s advantage over dense models as scale grows.
  • Shared experts: fix the count of shared experts and their size ratio relative to routed experts.
  • Load balancing: keep the load-balancing mechanism consistent across scales. Kimi K3, 2026 (Appendix C–D) uses Quantile Balancing (QB), setting per-expert router biases directly from target-load quantiles of router logits (aggregated across microbatches via histograms), which removes the manual bias-update step size required in earlier aux-loss-free methods. Because load-balancing strength affects both loss and expert-parallel throughput, keeping it consistent across the ladder is recommended.
  • Capacity factor and token dropping: match capacity factors between training and inference and monitor token-drop rates; DeepSeek-V3 drops zero tokens in both training and inference (§2.1.2).
  • Sparsity: ignoring memory and communication costs, Abnar et al., 2025 find that increasing sparsity (larger $N_{total}$) at fixed training FLOPs steadily lowers pretraining loss; at fixed $N_{total}$, loss follows a U-shaped curve in sparsity whose optimum shifts toward higher sparsity as compute grows. Most downstream tasks track pretraining loss regardless of sparsity, though reading-comprehension tasks such as CoQA and SQuAD favor higher active-parameter fractions. Ludziejewski et al., 2025 jointly fit expert count, $N_{active}$, and $D$ under memory constraints (up to 2.7B active / 5B total parameters).

4.5 Vocabulary Size and Precision

  • Vocabulary size: across 33M–3B models, Tao et al., 2024 show that optimal vocabulary size grows with compute budget and that most open-weight models use smaller-than-optimal vocabularies. Because changing vocabulary size alters both $O(Vd)$ compute and bytes per token, convert cross-vocabulary comparisons to byte-level BPB (§3.3).
  • Training precision: Kumar et al., 2024 (tested up to 1.7B parameters and 26B tokens) fold numerical precision and quantization into scaling laws: lower-precision training reduces effective parameter count, while higher training TPP makes models more sensitive to post-training quantization. Running ladder experiments directly in the target model’s training precision scheme is recommended (§10.3).

5. Experimental Design and Budget

5.1 Experimental Grid

A typical $(N, D)$ grid looks like this (sizes and multiples are illustrative):

  • Fitting points (●): small-to-medium runs across multiple token budgets used to fit the scaling law.
  • Holdout points (○): higher-scale or higher-compute runs withheld from fitting to test extrapolation accuracy.

Choose the layout to match your goal and budget: besides a full grid, common options include fixed-compute IsoFLOP sweeps (§5.3) and cheaper L-shape designs (§8.1). One pitfall to avoid: if every run sits at a single fixed TPP (say $D=20N$), $N$ and $D$ are perfectly collinear, making it impossible to separate their exponents in a bivariate fit $L(N,D)$.

5.2 Model Count, Scale Span, and Extrapolation Factor

Dimension Recommendation Reference
Number of model sizes At least 3–4 fitting sizes plus holdouts; more sizes stabilize slope estimates Choshen et al., 2025
Scale span Cover the direction of extrapolation; some model families follow power laws across a 34× span Choshen et al., 2025
Step ratio between sizes Geometric spacing (typically $2\times$–$4\times$) Standard practice

The extrapolation factor is the ratio between target compute and the largest fitting point. Delphi, for instance, fits on $3\times 10^{18}$–$3\times 10^{20}$ FLOPs and validates on $3\times$–$333\times$ holdouts; Llama 3, 2024 §3.2.1 fits on $6\times 10^{18}$–$10^{22}$ FLOPs (40M–16B) and extrapolates to $3.8\times 10^{25}$ FLOPs (roughly $3,800\times$). Larger leaps amplify both functional-form bias and late-stage instability risk; when the target run sits far above the largest holdout, adding an intermediate pilot run before launching the full run is recommended (§10.1).

To save compute, intermediate checkpoints along a single run (e.g., under WSD) can also be included in the fit. Because early loss trajectories are noisy, Choshen et al., 2025 find that dropping the first 10% of training or the first 10B tokens noticeably cuts extrapolation error; fix that cutoff beforehand, or pick it on a validation scale split following Lourie et al., 2026 (fit / validation / test tiers). Under a tight budget, whether to add one larger model size or run extra random seeds at small scales depends on the tradeoff between seed noise and extrapolation span (Choshen et al., 2025).

5.3 Token Horizons

Set token horizons around the target model’s expected TPP. Chinchilla’s IsoFLOP approach (Hoffmann et al., 2022) fixes several compute budgets $C$, trains models across a range of sizes $N$ at each $C$, and fits the minimum of each loss curve to read off $(N_{opt}, D_{opt})$.

Across over 400 models (70M–16B, 5B–500B tokens), Chinchilla obtains:

\[N_{opt}\propto C^a,\qquad D_{opt}\propto C^b,\qquad a\approx b\approx0.5\]

meaning compute-optimal training scales model size and training tokens roughly in proportion ($D/N\approx 20$, counting embeddings). Using the same IsoFLOP method, Llama 3 estimated an optimal size of ~402B at $3.8\times 10^{25}$ FLOPs and chose 405B (Llama 3 §3.2.1).

In practice, adjust token horizons to match the deployment regime:

  • Near compute-optimal: 0.5×–4× Chinchilla is common so the grid brackets 1× on both sides; Fantastic Optimizers uses 1×, 2×, 4×, and 8×.
  • Heavy overtraining: Gadre et al., 2024 verify power-law extrapolation up to 32× Chinchilla across 104 models. Testing up to 10,000 TPP across 47 models, Sardana et al., 2024 find that loss keeps falling, but Chinchilla curves fit only on low-TPP runs overestimate marginal token gains at high TPP. If the target run is heavily overtrained, include high-TPP runs on the ladder.
  • Accounting for inference cost: folding serving cost into total cost shifts the optimum toward smaller models trained far past Chinchilla (Sardana et al., 2024). Llama 3 405B sits near training compute optimality, whereas 8B and 70B were trained past 15T tokens to cut serving latency and memory (Llama 3 §1; see §10.4).
  • Data-constrained regime: when target tokens $D$ exceed deduplicated unique tokens $U$, log repetition epochs explicitly and include a repetition decay term when fitting (§7.3).

5.4 MoE Experimental Design

MoE models have more degrees of freedom than dense models, so it is cleanest to split experiments into three separate ladders and fit each on its own:

  1. Scale ladder: fix sparsity ratio $S$ and expert size, scale $N_{active}$ and $D$ together, and substitute $N_{active}$ for $N$ in the scaling law;
  2. Sparsity ladder: fix $N_{active}$, $D$, and active experts $E_{active}$, varying only total experts $E_{total}$;
  3. Granularity ladder: fix $N_{active}$ and $N_{total}$, adjusting expert size and $E_{active}$ inversely (Krajewski et al., 2024; Ludziejewski et al., 2025 model expert count, $N_{active}$, and $D$ jointly).

For example, Kimi K2, 2025 varies $E_{total}$ at fixed $N_{active}$ and training FLOPs to fit a sparsity scaling law: raising the sparsity ratio from 8 to 48 steadily reduces the FLOPs needed to reach a given loss, at the cost of higher cross-node communication and inference memory. Adding iso-$N_{active}$ or iso-FLOP dense baselines alongside the MoE scale ladder also shows how MoE’s compute multiplier over dense changes with scale.

6. Hyperparameter Search and Training Setup

6.1 Search Goals and Procedure

Lourie et al., 2026 and Step Law (Li et al., 2025) show that small models are sharply sensitive to hyperparameters: suboptimal settings raise loss and distort the fitted power-law slope, so clean scaling laws hold only along the Fully-Tuned Frontier. Large models, by contrast, exhibit a wide flat optimum (Wide Plateau): on a 156B-A7B model, Qwen3.8-Next, 2026 scaled LR up or down by $\sqrt{2}$ and raised BSZ by 25%, changing final training loss by no more than $7\times10^{-4}$.

Hyperparameter sensitivity roughly follows $\text{LR} > \text{BSZ} > \text{Schedule} > \text{WD}$, while numerical stability parameters like $\beta_2$ and $\epsilon$ can be fixed once the baseline is stable. Spend search budget primarily on small models and narrow down step by step (sizes are illustrative):

  1. Grid search on small models (e.g., 130M): sweep a 2D $(\eta, B)$ grid wide enough that the optimum sits strictly inside the interior rather than on the boundary;
  2. Slope check at medium scale (e.g., 500M): run a narrower grid to verify the power-law trend;
  3. Local check at larger scales: sample LR within $[\eta_{pred}/\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}\eta_{pred}]$ and BSZ within $[B_{opt}/2, 2B_{opt}]$ around the extrapolated values (§6.3), expanding only if a boundary point wins.

Picking the lowest loss across noisy trials introduces selection bias and overstates the winning configuration (Cawley & Talbot, 2010). Re-running the selected configuration with a fresh random seed and fitting on that rerun avoids this bias; also avoid early-stopping candidates based on early loss rankings, which often flip during cooldown (§6.6).

6.2 Parameterization and Cross-Optimizer Transfer

  • $\mu$P and width/depth transfer: $\mu$-Transfer (Yang et al., 2022) rescales per-layer initializations and LR multipliers so optimal LR transfers zero-shot across width (for non-zero weight decay adjustments, see Appendix G.1.2 of the paper and Power Lines). Along depth, Bordelon et al., 2023 scale residual branches by $1/\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ on top of $\mu$P to transfer across both width and depth on ResNets and ViTs; however, Tensor Programs VI (Yang et al., 2023) proves that Depth-$\mu$P holds only for single-layer residual blocks and breaks down theoretically for multi-layer nonlinear Transformer blocks. $\mu$P on its own also does not handle transfer across batch size or token horizon $D$. So while $\mu$P is theoretically elegant, industry teams still generally prefer fitting hyperparameter scaling laws empirically on a ladder rather than relying on $\mu$P transfer out of the box.
  • Cross-optimizer transfer: when scaling Muon to large LLMs, Liu et al., 2025 add weight decay and match the RMS norm of orthogonalized updates to AdamW’s typical 0.2–0.4 range (using 0.2), allowing Muon to reuse AdamW’s tuned LR and WD directly. When architecture and optimizer change together, however, Qwen3.8-Next, 2026 still observes shifts in optimal LR and BSZ after switching to Muon, requiring a fresh search on small models.

6.3 Scaling Laws for LR and BSZ

When a ladder uses only a single fixed $D/N$ ratio, optimal LR and BSZ can be fit directly as power laws of compute $C$:

\[\eta_{opt} = a \cdot C^{-\alpha}, \quad B_{opt} = b \cdot C^{\beta}\]

DeepSeek LLM, 2024 reports fitted coefficients in this form. Once a ladder spans multiple $D/N$ ratios, $C$ entangles $N$ and $D$, so the two variables need to be separated:

Formulation $\eta_{opt}$ $B_{opt}$ Scope and Findings
Step Law (Li et al., 2025) $c\cdot N^{-\alpha}D^{\beta}$ $0.58\,D^{0.571}$ Validated across 3,700+ runs across varying $D/N$; $B_{opt}$ depends mainly on $D$ and barely on $N$ (Appendix A.5)
Power Lines (Bergsma et al., 2025) Determined via timescale $\tau$ (§6.4) $\propto D^{0.4}$ (nearly independent of $N$) Connects $(\eta, B, \lambda)$ through a single EMA timescale $\tau$
Token Horizons (Bjorck et al., 2024) $c \cdot N^{-\alpha} D^{-\beta}$ Fixed BSZ At fixed $N$ and fixed BSZ, longer horizons $D$ favor a lower peak LR

Notice that $\eta_{opt}$ has opposite signs on the $D$ exponent in Token Horizons ($D^{-\beta}$) vs. Step Law ($D^{+\beta}$). Their setups differ: Token Horizons holds batch size fixed, whereas Step Law jointly tunes BSZ at each $D$ and scales $B_{opt}\propto D^{0.571}$. Whether batch-size co-scaling fully accounts for the sign flip remains open, so check against holdouts under your own schedule and BSZ rule.

For MoE models, sparsity makes it hard to build tiny MoE ladders, so running full hyperparameter sweeps directly on MoE is expensive. In practice, changing the attention mechanism or switching from dense to MoE barely alters the LR scaling exponents, and optimal LR mostly shifts by a constant factor. A practical compute-saving workflow is to first fit $\eta_{\text{dense}}^{\ast}(N, D)$ on an inexpensive dense ladder, run just a few small MoE points to fit a scalar transfer factor $c_{\text{MoE}}$, and obtain the MoE LR law as $\eta_{\text{MoE}}^{\ast} = c_{\text{MoE}}\,\eta_{\text{dense}}^{\ast}$.

For batch size, both Step Law and Power Lines find that $B_{opt}$ grows as a power law of training tokens $D$ (exponent ~0.4–0.57) and is largely independent of model size $N$. Runs of different model sizes sharing the same token budget can therefore start with the same BSZ, cutting a search dimension.

Power Lines also measures the critical batch size $B_{crit}\propto D_{min}^{0.5}$ (where $D_{min}$ is the minimum tokens needed to reach a target loss in the small-batch limit). As shown in Figure 5, tokens $D$ and optimization steps $S$ required to hit a given loss form a hyperbola: training at $B=B_{crit}$ costs $2D_{min}$ tokens, and pushing batch size past $B_{crit}$ saves very few steps while burning substantially more compute.

Schaipp, 2026 estimates that the batch-size window incurring at most 5% compute overhead spans roughly $4\times$, or $[B_{opt}/2, 2B_{opt}]$, which is wide enough at medium and large scales for a small local check or rounding to hardware-friendly parallelism multiples. Large runs also sometimes ramp batch size up early in training (for instance, Llama 3 §3.4.1 steps BSZ from 4M up to 16M tokens, and Nemotron-4, 2024 uses a similar ramp) to balance early convergence speed and stability.

6.4 Weight Decay

Across most industry pretraining runs, weight decay is simply held at a constant (most commonly $\lambda=0.1$) without scaling across $N$ or $D$: for example, Kimi K2, 2025 fixes $\lambda=0.1$ throughout, OLMo 3, 2025 uses a fixed AdamW $\lambda$ excluding embeddings, and Qwen3, 2025 extrapolates only LR and BSZ while keeping WD constant.

Several academic papers explore WD scaling laws: Han et al., 2026 find that the pretraining-loss-optimal $\lambda_{opt}$ decreases at higher TPP (while noting that slightly larger WD preserves effective weight rank and post-training plasticity), and Power Lines ties $(\eta, B, \lambda)$ together via the weight EMA timescale $\tau = B/(\eta\lambda D)$, adjusting $\lambda \propto B/D$ at fixed $\mu$P $\eta$ to keep $\tau_{opt}$ constant. Whether scaling WD actually pays off at frontier scale remains open; in practice, fixing $\lambda=0.1$ on the ladder is usually sufficient.

6.5 LR Schedule and Cooldown

Two learning-rate schedules dominate pretraining:

  • Cosine schedule: decays smoothly along a cosine curve from warmup down to an end learning rate $\eta_{\text{end}}$, suited to runs with a predetermined step budget.
  • WSD (Warmup-Stable-Decay): holds LR constant for most of training before annealing rapidly at the end (River Valley (Wen et al., 2024) explains the stable and decay phases via loss-landscape geometry; Tissue et al., 2024 and Wang et al., 2025 study how decay shapes and decay fractions transfer across scales). WSD’s practical advantage is that multiple decay branches can fork off a single stable trajectory at different horizons without re-running the trunk.

Three empirical lessons from industry practice matter when choosing $\eta_{\text{end}}$ and the schedule:

  • Do not decay $\eta_{\text{end}}$ to 0 during PT: base pretraining is almost always followed by continued pretraining (CPT / CT) and post-training, so keeping $\eta_{\text{end}}$ at a small non-zero value (e.g., $3\times 10^{-5}$) leaves room for subsequent training stages to make progress.
  • Fixing absolute $\eta_{\text{end}}$ beats fixing the ratio $\eta_{\text{end}}/\eta_{\text{peak}}$: earlier work (such as Kaplan et al., 2020 and DeepSeek LLM, 2024) commonly fixed $\eta_{\text{end}}/\eta_{\text{peak}}=0.1$, coupling the end LR to the peak LR. Raising $\eta_{\text{peak}}$ then drags $\eta_{\text{end}}$ up with it, leaving too much gradient noise unaveraged at the end of training and preventing $\eta_{\text{peak}}$ from reaching higher, better values. Fixing an absolute $\eta_{\text{end}}$ (as in Step Law) unlocks higher optimal $\eta_{\text{peak}}$ and lower final loss.
  • Properly tuned Cosine outperforms WSD: at matched $(N, D)$, Cosine and WSD have noticeably different optimal $(\eta, B)$; once $\eta_{\text{end}}$ is fixed and hyperparameters are tuned independently for each schedule, Cosine favors a higher peak LR ($\eta_{\text{cosine}}^{\ast} > \eta_{\text{WSD}}^{\ast}$) and consistently reaches lower final pretraining loss than WSD (Kimi K3, 2026 §3.2).

If the full-scale run uses either of the following end-of-training steps, mirror them on the ladder as well:

  • Cooldown data shift (Continued Training / Cooldown): switching to higher-quality data mixtures during LR decay (Nemotron-4, 2024; OLMo 2, 2025; OLMo 3, 2025; Nemotron 3 Super, 2026); see §11.
  • Weight averaging: averaging independently fine-tuned branches from a shared checkpoint (Model Soups) or maintaining a sliding average along a single trajectory (LAWA; Sanyal et al., 2024). On 1.3B and 13B models, Model Merging (ByteDance Seed, 2025) uses checkpoint averaging (PMA) along the WSD stable phase as a cheap proxy for LR annealing; empirically, however, weight averaging only approximates LR decay and generally does not capture the full loss drop of an actual cooldown.

6.6 Comparing Recipes Fairly

Keep three rules in mind when comparing optimizers, architectures, or LR schedules on a ladder:

  • Match tuning effort on both sides: Fantastic Optimizers (Wen et al., 2025) show that comparing a tuned new optimizer against default, untuned AdamW hyperparameters substantially overstates gains; once both sides are properly tuned, the gap shrinks considerably. The same applies when comparing LR schedules: tune hyperparameters separately for each schedule (as in the Cosine vs. WSD comparison in §6.5).
  • Compare at the end of cooldown: Fantastic Optimizers observe that loss rankings during the stable phase frequently flip during LR decay, so mid-run rankings can be misleading.
  • Check whether gains decay with scale: in Fantastic Optimizers’ 8× Chinchilla runs, matrix-preconditioned optimizers’ token-efficiency lead over AdamW shrank from $1.4\times$ at 0.1B to $1.1\times$ at 1.2B (and token-efficiency gains do not automatically translate into wall-clock speedups). Always test candidate improvements across multiple model sizes to see if the gain narrows with scale.

7. Data Ladders

I do not come from a data background and my familiarity with data pipelines is limited, so this section draws on public papers to briefly cover the three data questions directly tied to scaling ladders: evaluating new data sources (§7.1), searching multi-domain mixtures (§7.2), and multi-epoch repetition under data constraints (§7.3).

7.1 Data Quality and Source Evaluation

Comparing pretraining corpora in DeepSeek LLM, 2024 highlights two effects:

  • Higher-quality data shifts optimal compute allocation toward larger model sizes $N$;
  • Fitted scaling law coefficients differ noticeably across corpora and should not be copied across datasets.

Whenever the underlying corpus version changes, re-verify scaling law parameters. In Kimi K3, 2026 §3.2, for example, updating the architecture, corpus, and training recipe together prompted refitting BSZ, LR, optimal TPP, and aspect ratio.

Routine screening of an individual new data source does not require re-running a full $(N, D)$ grid; three low-cost methods are commonly used:

  • Checkpoint micro-annealing: OLMo 2, 2025 and OLMo 3, 2025 branch from a mid-to-late trunk checkpoint, mix the candidate source with the base corpus for a short cooldown, and measure downstream shifts directly.
  • Small-model single-domain ablations: Kimi K3 §3.1 runs single-domain ablations on a fixed small model to set per-domain sampling weights.
  • Targeted synthetic-data ablations: Nemotron 3 Ultra, 2026 mixes synthetic legal reasoning data into Nemotron 3 Nano, 2025 pretraining, lifting LegalBench average accuracy from 64.6 to 74.7.

7.2 Data Mixture Ladders

A data mixture ladder holds model size $N$, token budget $D$, and optimizer hyperparameters fixed while varying sampling weights $\mathbf{w}=(w_1,\ldots,w_m)$ across $m$ data buckets. Combining Olmix, 2026, OLMo 3, 2025 §3.4.4, and the Marin Data Pipeline, 2026, a typical workflow looks like this:

  1. Define buckets and count unique tokens: partition data by source, domain, or instance-level attributes (e.g., educational value, code, language, safety; Qwen3, 2025), and recount deduplicated unique tokens $N_j$ in every bucket whenever filtering thresholds change (Marin Data Pipeline, 2026). If training shifts mixtures across stages (for example, Nemotron 3 Super, 2026 emphasizes diversity in the first 80% of its 25T tokens and high-quality sources in the final 20%), optimize each stage’s mixture separately (§11).
  2. Establish simple baselines: compare against natural token-count proportions and epoch-capped UniMax; Marin notes that poorly constrained mixture regressions can easily underperform both baselines.
  3. Train small proxy models:
    • Proxy size: at 5× Chinchilla horizons, proxy models of 15M parameters and above achieve >0.89 Spearman rank correlation with a 1B target model across mixtures, dropping to 0.73 at 1M (Olmix); OLMo 3 uses 30M proxies trained for 3B tokens.
    • Proportional pool downsampling: because small proxies consume far fewer tokens, sampling directly from the full corpus avoids the multi-epoch repetition that the large run will actually hit. Marin therefore downsamples each bucket’s pool in proportion to the proxy’s active parameters so that small runs at 791 tokens/active param experience the exact same repetition epochs as the full model. In my view, keeping repetition epochs consistent across all ladder sizes is very important, especially when studying components like n-gram modules that depend heavily on corpus size, repetition frequency, and memorization/overfitting; without proportional pool scaling, a small model sees well under 1 epoch on the full pool and behaves nothing like a multi-epoch large run.
    • Number of sweeps and model-merging alternatives: fitting a log-linear mixture model typically takes at least $3(m+1)$ proxy runs sampled from a Dirichlet distribution centered on natural proportions. DeMix, 2026 instead trains one component model per bucket and evaluates arbitrary mixtures via weighted parameter merging, achieving higher rank correlation than retraining small proxies from scratch.
  4. Fit and solve under constraints: fitting separate log-linear models per downstream task before aggregating achieves much higher held-out mixture correlation (0.983) than fitting a single aggregate score directly (0.866; Olmix). Because overweighting a small bucket causes excessive repetition and drives loss back up (Marin), enforce per-bucket epoch caps $w_j \le k N_j / R$ ($R$ = target token budget, $k$ = max epochs; §7.3) alongside a KL penalty toward natural proportions (e.g., $\lambda=0.05$), then verify the resulting mixture against natural sampling and UniMax at medium scale.
  5. Incremental mixture reuse after corpus updates: when only a few buckets are added or updated, Olmix’s mixture reuse freezes relative weights among unchanged buckets and re-runs only sweeps touching the updated ones. Across 5 update rounds scaling to 64 domains at 1B/100B, this cut proxy runs by 74% while retaining 95% of the gain of a full re-sweep (+11.6% over natural proportions), powering three data iterations in OLMo 3.

7.3 Data-Constrained Training and Repetition

When target training tokens $D$ exceed total unique tokens $U$, training enters the multi-epoch regime. Run global cross-source deduplication before counting epochs: global deduplication in the Marin Data Pipeline, 2026 uncovered heavy overlap across public datasets, with the largest share coming from Nemotron-CC and its synthetic rewrites.

Assuming exponentially decaying marginal value from each extra pass, Muennighoff et al., 2023 define the effective data size $D^\prime$:

\[D^{\prime} = U_D + U_D \cdot R_D^{\ast} \left(1 - e^{-R_D / R_D^{\ast}}\right)\]

where $U_D$ is unique tokens, $R_D$ is the number of extra repetitions beyond the first pass ($R_D=0$ for 1 epoch), and $R_D^\ast$ is the repetition half-life parameter (empirically ~4). As $R_D\to\infty$, effective data saturates at $U_D(1+R_D^\ast)$.

Key findings on multi-epoch limits and mitigations include:

  • Overfitting scales with both $N$ and unique pool size $U$: Yan et al., 2025 show analytically in linear regression that the optimal epoch count grows logarithmically with dataset size; empirically, Lovelace et al., 2026 and Xue et al., 2023 find that larger $N$, smaller $U$, and more repetitions all worsen overfitting. At fixed $U$, scaling $N$ aggressively worsens memorization and shifts compute-optimal allocation toward smaller $N$.
  • Data quality and dropout: Xue et al., 2023 find that higher data quality at fixed unique token count does not prevent multi-epoch overfitting, whereas adding moderate dropout (v2 Table 4) substantially mitigates it.
  • Mixing scarce domains into a large general corpus: a small target domain tolerates far more epochs when mixed into a large general background corpus than when trained on alone. Across 2,000+ runs, Sedova et al., 2026 show positive returns up to 15–20 epochs on scarce target domains in mixtures, with repetition-aware mixture scaling laws extrapolating cleanly from small to large models.
  • Quality-tiered epoch caps: when continuing joint pretraining from a late Kimi K2 checkpoint, Kimi K2.5, 2026 enforces strict per-source epoch caps; OLMo 3 (§3.4.4, Appendix A.2.5) upsamples only high-quality buckets up to 7 epochs, outperforming aggressive hard filtering.
  • Synthetic rephrasing over raw repetition: on SimpleQA, Kimi K2, 2025 §2.2 compares repeating raw text 10 epochs (23.76), rephrasing once and training 10 epochs (27.39), and generating 10 distinct rephrasings trained 1 epoch each (28.94); production runs cap knowledge-corpus rephrasing at 2 passes, a practice carried into Kimi K3 §3.1. Track rephrased tokens separately from raw unique tokens when accounting.

8. Fitting Loss Scaling Laws

Before fitting $L(N,D)$, double-check three prerequisites (Porian et al., 2024): FLOPs $C$ includes the output head while $N_{\text{body}}$ is logged separately (§3.1), warmup steps scale proportionally with total training tokens (§4.1), and every grid point has been tuned onto the Fully-Tuned Frontier when solving for optimal allocation or comparing methods (§6.1).

8.1 Functional Forms

If all runs share a single fixed $D/N$ ratio, a 1D power law in compute $L(C) = A/C^\gamma + E$ suffices. Predicting across varying $D/N$ ratios or solving for optimal $(N, D)$ allocation requires a 2D surface in $N$ and $D$. The standard starting point is Chinchilla’s additive form:

\[L(N,D) = \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} + E\]

This additive ansatz assumes $N$ and $D$ act independently ($\partial^2L/\partial N\partial D\equiv0$). Across wide $(N, D)$ grids, however, Chinchilla’s residuals form a clear saddle pattern growing toward all four corners (Figure 7 left), and empirical mixed partials remain consistently negative, showing that model capacity and data horizon interact.

Two recent formulations capture this $(N, D)$ coupling:

  • Farseer (Li et al., 2025): lets both the coefficient and exponent of the data term vary with $N$, using 9 parameters in total;
  • Skaling (Videau et al., 2026): wraps the additive terms in a single outer coupling exponent $k$, using 6 parameters (recovering Chinchilla at $k=1$, with empirical fits around $k \approx 0.31$–$0.45$):
\[L(N,D)=\left(\frac{A}{N^{\alpha}}+\frac{B}{D^{\beta}}\right)^{k}+E\]

Skaling adds just one parameter over Chinchilla while preserving closed-form $(N_{opt}, D_{opt})$ solutions; across both the Farseer and SK-Grid benchmarks, 6-parameter Skaling achieves lower interpolation and extrapolation error than both Chinchilla and 9-parameter Farseer (Skaling Table 1; Figure 7 center and right), making it a strong default choice.

A second practical benefit of Skaling is avoiding full-grid sweeps. As shown in Figure 8, running an L-shape layout (many model sizes $N$ at small $D$, plus many token horizons $D$ at small $N$) costs only $1/5$–$1/10$ the compute of a dense grid yet extrapolates into the large-$(N,D)$ corner nearly as accurately as a full grid under Skaling, whereas Chinchilla’s additive form degrades sharply on L-shape samples.

8.2 Loss Trajectory and Annealing Scaling Laws

Tissue et al., 2024 model loss along training step $s$ as a functional of the LR schedule:

\[\hat L(s) = L_0 + A \cdot S_1(s)^{-\alpha} - C \cdot S_2(s)\]

where $S_1(s)=\sum_{i\leq s}\eta_i$ is cumulative learning rate area and $S_2(s)$ captures cumulative annealing weighted by a forgetting kernel. This lets you fit entire loss trajectories using intermediate evaluations rather than final endpoints alone, and predicts curves across cosine schedules, WSD, and re-warmed continued training (§4.7 of the paper). Note that the formulation assumes a fixed peak LR, so extrapolating peak LR across scales still relies on the hyperparameter scaling laws in §6.3.

Personal experience: replacing the single-exponential forgetting kernel in $S_2(s)$ with a power-law kernel $K(\Delta) = (1+\Delta)^{-p}$ generally produces cleaner fits than the paper’s original kernel.

8.3 Numerical Fitting Details

Fitting power laws with plain nonlinear least squares easily gets trapped in poor local minima: exponents $(\alpha, \beta)$ and prefactors $(A, B)$ are strongly collinear, so minor shifts in initialization or outlier weighting can swing extrapolated $(N_{opt}, D_{opt})$ by several-fold (Besiroglu et al., 2024; (Mis)Fitting (Li et al., 2025)). Standard practice follows Chinchilla: reparameterize prefactors in log space ($a=\log A, b=\log B, e=\log E$), evaluate $\log \hat{L}(N,D)$ via log-sum-exp, minimize Huber loss on $\log L$ (e.g., $\delta=10^{-3}$) to dampen outliers, and run multi-start L-BFGS across an initialization grid before picking the lowest-objective solution.

9. Downstream Task Prediction

Beyond pretraining loss, teams naturally want to use a pretraining ladder to forecast hero-run scores on downstream benchmarks. I do not work directly on downstream prediction myself, so my perspective here is limited; in practice, however, task losses and discrete accuracies are far noisier and much harder to extrapolate than pretraining loss (Schaeffer et al., 2024 analyze how distractor probability mass and argmax thresholding add substantial prediction noise on multiple-choice benchmarks). Both academia and industry are still actively experimenting with new approaches, and no single consensus method has emerged yet.

A few representative lines of work illustrate current thinking:

Approach Core Idea Representative Work
General Loss $\to$ Task Score Extrapolate pretraining loss from $(N,D)$, then map loss to benchmark score via a monotonic power law or sigmoid Gadre et al., 2024; Delphi
$(N,D) \to$ Task NLL $\to$ Acc Two-stage fit: extrapolate gold-answer negative log-likelihood (NLL) vs. compute, then fit a sigmoid from NLL to accuracy Bhagia et al., 2024 (OLMo Task Ladder); Llama 3 §3.2.1
Difficulty-clustered subsets Cluster benchmark items by small-model pass rates, extrapolate compute curves on subsets with clean upward signal, then map back to full-benchmark accuracy COD (Xu et al., 2026, v4); GPT-4 Technical Report
Hint-assisted evaluation Give small models graded hints at eval time to lift them off 0%, jointly fit accuracy over compute and hint strength, and extrapolate unhinted large-model accuracy Hinted Scaling Laws, 2026

For standard two-stage prediction, Llama 3 §3.2.1 is a classic example: stage one fits a linear trend from $\log\text{FLOPs}$ to normalized gold-choice NLL on ladder runs at $\le 10^{22}$ FLOPs, and stage two combines ladder checkpoints with Llama 2 models to fit a sigmoid from NLL to accuracy, closely matching 405B performance on ARC-Challenge.

On reasoning benchmarks like MATH, AIME, and GSM8K, the main bottleneck is that small models score near 0% across the board, leaving no upward trend to extrapolate. Two recent methods get around this zero-score floor from opposite sides:

First, clustering items by difficulty on the benchmark side (COD, Xu et al., 2026, v4): using repeated-sampling pass rates across small models to cluster problems by difficulty, COD drops both zero-score items (0% across all small models) and already-saturated easy items, fits a bi-exponential curve on medium-difficulty clusters with clear monotonic signal to extrapolate to target compute, and maps cluster scores back to full-benchmark accuracy via a learned calibration curve. Across 8 benchmarks, COD predicts 70B accuracy within a 1.55 percentage-point mean absolute error (Figure 9; cross-architecture transfer from dense ladders to MoE roughly doubles error to ~3.11 percentage points).

Second, lowering task difficulty via hints on the evaluation side (Hinted Scaling Laws, van Adrichem et al., 2026): instead of filtering items, it turns the benchmark into a continuum of easier variants parameterized by hint strength $H$ (revealing the first $H$ fraction of reference solution steps in Truncation, randomly unmasking $H$ fraction of solution words in Masking, or interpolating logits with a strong teacher at weight $H$ in Logit mixing), so models that score 0% unhinted produce smooth non-zero accuracy curves (Figure 10 left). Because hint strength $H$ and log compute $C$ trade off nearly linearly in lifting accuracy, fitting a joint floored sigmoid in $(C, H)$ on just the smallest ladder runs and evaluating at $H=0$ forecasts unhinted accuracy at scale. On the Delphi pretraining ladder, fitting only $\le 10^{20}$ FLOPs runs predicts GSM8K accuracy at $10^{23}$ FLOPs within 3 percentage points, compared to a 13 percentage-point miss from unhinted extrapolation (Figure 10 right).

10. Extrapolation Validation

10.1 Holdouts and Joint Validation

Place holdout runs along the actual direction of extrapolation (larger $N$, longer $D$, or higher compute $C$) and span multiple extrapolation factors when budget permits (see §1.1, §5.2). Once a holdout set is used to revise the recipe or functional form, it effectively becomes a validation set, and unbiased error estimates require fresh holdouts. When the target run sits orders of magnitude above the largest holdout, running a medium-scale pilot with the final frozen recipe beforehand checks that the loss trajectory stays inside the predicted band.

Architecture, optimizer, hyperparameters, and data mixtures are usually ablated one at a time, but gains from individual changes are rarely additive (Marin Follow-up; OLMo 3, 2025 Appendix A.2.5). Choices interact: a stronger optimizer can change multi-epoch overfitting speed, and a new data mixture can shift optimal LR. Before locking in the final recipe, combine all winning changes and run a joint validation at the target TPP and repetition count.

10.2 Stability Stress Tests

Shallow, short small-model runs rarely surface late-stage numerical overflows, loss spikes, or router collapse that appear at scale. Qwen3.8-Next, 2026 runs high-LR stress tests on medium-scale models, pushing LR to $2\times$ and $4\times$ the predicted optimum to compare stability head-to-head across candidate architectures or optimizers under elevated optimization pressure.

Short high-LR stress tests still do not replace continuous monitoring late into a long run. In Nemotron 3 Ultra, 2026 §2.7, two late-training divergences occurred: the first came from low-precision gradient underflow/overflow in the output layer and was resolved by casting output-layer gradients back to FP32; the cause of the second remained undetermined and was mitigated by starting cooldown early. Track per-layer gradient norms, activation maxima, and loss spikes throughout full-scale runs, plus expert load balance and token-drop rates for MoE.

10.3 Aligning Training Frameworks and Hardware

Small ladder runs and large flagship runs often execute on different stacks: small sweeps commonly use FSDP with standard kernels, whereas large runs switch to frameworks like Megatron with hybrid TP/PP/EP parallelism, fused kernels, low-precision training (such as FP8 in DeepSeek-V3, 2024 §3.3), and frequent checkpoint restarts (Llama 3, 2024 §3.3.4).

In my own experience, even with the exact same architecture, optimizer, and data, running on different training frameworks often produces noticeably different losses and internal training metrics (such as per-layer grad norms and activations); switching GPU generations (for instance, Hopper vs. Blackwell GPUs) can also shift loss by around 0.01.

Ideally, a research ladder should run on the exact same training framework and GPU generation as the full-scale run. When unifying them is not practical, align pretraining loss, validation/test loss, benchmark evaluation losses and scores, and all internal training observables as closely as possible on identical configurations.

10.4 Wall-Clock Speed and Inference Cost

Theoretical FLOP reductions do not automatically translate 1-to-1 into wall-clock speedups. The Marin Follow-up distinguishes theoretical efficiency from realized efficiency, and Nemotron 3 Ultra, 2026 likewise benchmarks precision and kernel choices by measured cluster throughput, especially for MoE where cross-node EP all-to-all communication adds zero FLOPs yet directly governs step time.

For models headed into production, factor serving cost into $(N, D)$ selection as well (see §5.3). Sardana et al., 2024 model pretraining cost jointly with prefill and decode serving compute; in practice, combine expected prompt/output lengths, latency targets (TTFT / TPOT), memory limits, and serving quantization precision with measured cluster throughput when choosing model size and token horizon.

11. Multi-Stage Training Ladders

Modern LLMs routinely train across multiple stages: general pretraining (PT), followed by high-quality continued training (CT / Mid-training) and long-context extension. Because data mixtures and LR schedules change across stages, scaling law coefficients fit on PT cannot be plugged directly into later stages (Qwen3, 2025; OLMo 3, 2025).

When CT / Mid-training requires quantitative forecasts for hyperparameters, mixture weights, or stage token budgets, build a dedicated CT ladder on top of the PT ladder: initialize each model size from its corresponding PT checkpoint (keeping a non-zero PT $\eta_{\text{end}}$; see §6.5), fit stage-specific scaling curves, and track gains on target capabilities alongside retention on core general benchmarks.

Keep in mind that CT outcomes depend on the state of the starting checkpoint: two PT checkpoints with similar validation loss can respond differently to the exact same CT recipe if they differ in end LR, prior data repetition counts, or optimizer momentum states. Match how small-scale starting checkpoints are produced, including whether LR re-warms or optimizer states reset at the stage boundary, to the target full-scale pipeline.

As for the final long-context extension stage, its token budget is a tiny fraction of total pretraining and teams do not necessarily build a standalone scaling ladder for it; I also do not work on long-context training or evaluation myself, and it sits outside the main focus of this post, so I skip a detailed discussion here (for long-sequence FLOPs accounting see §3.2, and for evaluation benchmarks and attention tradeoffs see RULER, 2024 and Chen et al., 2025).

12. Practical Checklist

The checklist below is meant only as an empirical rule-of-thumb reference rather than an exhaustive spec. Even at frontier labs, building a scaling ladder inevitably surfaces subtle implementation bugs, and what separates strong executions is usually catching and fixing more bugs faster. Here are the highest-leverage checks distilled from earlier sections:

  • Separate parameter and FLOPs accounting: fit $L(N,D)$ using non-embedding backbone parameters $N_{\text{body}}$, while adding both the output head and sequence-length attention terms back into FLOPs $C$ (§3.1, §3.2).
  • Scale warmup and eval intervals as fractions of training: avoid fixed step counts across scales; compare main next-token cross-entropy on a shared validation set, and use BPB across tokenizers (§3.3, §4.1, §4.5).
  • Tune small runs thoroughly and compare after full cooldown: spend search budget putting small grid points onto the Fully-Tuned Frontier; tune each candidate architecture, optimizer, or schedule independently and judge rankings at the end of LR decay (§6.1, §6.6).
  • LR schedule and MoE LR transfer: keep a non-zero PT $\eta_{\text{end}}$ for CT, and fix absolute $\eta_{\text{end}}$ rather than fixing $\eta_{\text{end}}/\eta_{\text{peak}}$ (§6.5); for MoE, fit the LR scaling law on a cheap dense ladder first and estimate the scalar multiplier $c_{\text{MoE}}$ on a few small MoE runs (§6.3).
  • Match data repetition epochs across scales: for multi-epoch or data-mixture experiments, downsample bucket pools proportionally with model scale and account for repetition decay when fitting (§7.2, §7.3).
  • Use coupled functional forms and drop noisy early steps: prefer Skaling for 2D $(N, D)$ fits (paired with L-shape sampling when budget is tight); drop the first ~10% of steps when fitting intermediate checkpoints, and optimize log-space Huber loss with multi-start L-BFGS (§5.2, §8.1, §8.3).
  • Multi-tier holdouts, joint validation, and high-LR stress tests: reserve untouched holdouts along the extrapolation trajectory; validate combined recipe changes together, and stress-test new architectures or optimizers at $2\times / 4\times$ LR (§10.1, §10.2).
  • Align training frameworks and GPUs: run the ladder on the same framework and GPU generation as the target model whenever possible; otherwise align losses, benchmark scores, and internal training observables on matched runs (§10.3).
