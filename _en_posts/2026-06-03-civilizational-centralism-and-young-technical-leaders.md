Lately I've been thinking about a question: how should the future of Chinese political philosophy be imagined?

This question sounds distant, but it actually appears every day around us. On social media, in classroom discussions, and in public narratives, we are increasingly accustomed to describing the world as zero-sum competition: civilizations are camps, national interest is the highest morality, and most external criticism is attributed to hostility. Much of this is packaged as traditional confidence, but I always feel something is off. It seems to borrow the name of tradition to accept a very untraditional logic of power.

Liu Qing once made a sharp judgment when discussing "tianxia" and "new cosmopolitanism": some Chinese people's worldview today seems to "imitate a barbaric late-19th-century West that has been eliminated." This sentence struck me. The danger is not in learning from the West, but in learning only the roughest aspects of Western history: national interest above all, the law of the jungle, colonial competition, social Darwinism, and the victor proving everything.

If China ends up learning only this set, it cannot be said to surpass the West. It is merely replicating, in another language, the part of Western modernity that most deserves reflection.

## The Danger of Civilizational Centrism

Much of what is called civilizational confidence today actually stops at civilizational centrism.

Civilizational self-awareness is not this. Civilizational self-awareness means knowing where one comes from, knowing one's historical experience and spiritual resources, and also knowing that these resources are not perfect and must undergo the test and transformation of the modern world. Civilizational centrism, on the other hand, imagines one's own civilization as naturally correct, naturally superior, and naturally immune to external criticism. Civilization should be a process of learning and self-correction, but here it is compressed into a closed identity.

I roughly see several manifestations.

One is treating historical length as moral qualification. It seems that the older a civilization is, the more it automatically possesses the authority to interpret the modern world. But a long history only shows that there is much experience; it does not automatically show that institutions are legitimate, values are superior, or judgments are more reliable.

Another is treating cultural difference as a firewall. When external criticism appears, it is said to be "not understanding China," "Western bias," or "cultural colonization." Of course, Eurocentrism really exists, and colonial experience cannot be easily glossed over. But if all criticism can be deflected by cultural difference, civilization is no longer a resource for self-reflection, but an excuse to refuse reflection.

There is also a way of imagining the world as a field of victory and defeat between civilizations. Chinese civilization, Western civilization, Islamic civilization, and Indian civilization are placed on the same large arena, as if history ultimately only cares about who overwhelms whom. This kind of statement looks grand, but it is actually very narrow. It merely expands identity competition between individuals into identity competition between civilizations.

A civilization with real confidence should not fear foreign ideas. It should be able to digest external things and then express its own things in a better way. If culture cannot encounter the other, it is only self-repetition; if civilization cannot accept criticism, it is just a larger tribe.

## Which West Did We Learn From

Therefore, the question is never simply "whether to read Western philosophy." More specifically, which West did we read?

There is a West that is the West of 19th-century imperialism, colonialism, realpolitik, and social Darwinism. In this West, the state is the highest entity, force is the final arbiter, the elimination of the weak is described as a historical law, and the civilizing mission is often just a wrapper for expansion and domination. This is certainly part of Western history and cannot be downplayed.

But Western thought has also been reflecting on these things internally. It reflects on colonialism, racism, capitalist inequality, the violence of the nation-state, the tyranny of the majority, and the domination of technological rationality over people. Modern Western thought includes realpolitik and sovereignty theories like Hobbes and Schmitt, but also traditions of freedom, dignity, public reason, recognition, and cosmopolitanism like Kant, Mill, Rawls, Arendt, and Taylor.

If we only learn from the former West, the result will be awkward: verbally anti-Western, but actually imitating the most barbaric historical stage of the West; verbally adhering to Chinese tradition, but actually discarding the broader "tianxia" breadth in Chinese tradition.

This is also how I understand Liu Qing's words. He is not saying that China should naively give up national interest, nor that China should accept any form of colonialism and domination. Getting rid of domination is certainly a basic premise. But when China already has sufficient national strength, the question cannot always stop at "how not to be bullied." The harder question is: after not being bullied, can we only understand the world in terms of bullying and being bullied?

Prosperity and strength are important. Without them, there is no basic security, nor the realistic conditions for talking about higher ideals. But prosperity and strength are not the end of civilization. After a country becomes strong, how power is constrained becomes a problem; after civilization gains confidence, how to treat others becomes a problem; after intellectuals gain influence, they must also ask clearly what worldview they bring into society.

## "Tianxia" Is Not an Answer, but a Question

Many would say that the Chinese tradition already contains the tianxia worldview, harmony in diversity, seeking common ground while reserving differences, and mutual appreciation of beauty. These are certainly important resources. But I am now more wary of taking these words directly as answers.

Tianxia has at least two sides.

Its good side is that it provides a scale beyond the nation-state. The modern nation-state divides the world into sovereign units, each calculating its own security, interests, and development first. But the problems of the era of globalization can hardly be fully handled by such units. Climate, AI, war, finance, supply chains, biotechnology, and cybersecurity are not things any single country can solve alone. In this sense, tianxia reminds us that the world itself should be an object of political philosophy.

Its troublesome side is also obvious. Historically, tianxia often carried a structure of center and periphery, Huaxia and barbarians, civilization and submission. Without critique, tianxia can easily become a milder, prettier new centrism. On the surface it claims to embrace all, but in reality it still assumes itself at the center, with others merely outsiders waiting to be incorporated into this order.

So I am reluctant to describe the future as a "new tianxia." In contrast, Liu Qing's "new cosmopolitanism" is more accurate. It retains the world scale of tianxia but rejects Sinocentrism; it acknowledges the intellectual resources of the Chinese tradition but does not sanctify them; it opposes Eurocentrism and also opposes replacing Eurocentrism with Sinocentrism.

What I want to pursue is a world where different civilizations build together, correct each other, and bear the consequences together. This world should not be defined by China alone, nor should it continue to be defined by the West alone.

## Why universal values need local roots

Liu Qing mentioned Charles Taylor, and I think this is unavoidable.

If many ideas enter China only as external principles, they are easily understood as alien. Freedom, rule of law, dignity, rights, equality, and public reason, if always described as "Western values," will be psychologically placed outside China. Those who support them are easily seen as not Chinese enough, while those who oppose them can easily package themselves as truly Chinese.

This is actually very bad.

For a political idea to persuade people, it must not only be logically valid but also enter a society's self-understanding. What Taylor calls the social imaginary refers precisely to the background imagination people have about common life. We live by theory, but also by stories, identities, memories, and familiar moral language.

This is why Chinese people should not only read Western philosophy but also re-understand the Chinese tradition. The purpose is not to prove that "ancient China already had everything," but to give modern values comprehensible roots within China. For example, "rights" cannot simply be replaced by "people as the foundation," but the tradition of people as the foundation can help us understand why power should be responsible to the people; "cosmopolitanism" cannot simply be equated with tianxia, but the tianxia tradition can help us understand why national interest should not be the highest morality; "dignity" is not a direct product of traditional ritual governance, but the Confucian view of human moral agency can engage in dialogue with modern views of dignity.

The problem is not a binary choice between China and the West. What I care more about is how to transform the still vital parts of the Chinese tradition into a normative language that can constrain modern power, protect modern individuals, and respond to global risks.

## The responsibility of intellectuals is not self-justification

These questions look like the work of political philosophers, but now I also place them on every intellectual.

Knowledge has never been a mere tool for individual advancement. A person who reads, researches, writes, or does technology will certainly gain career opportunities and social position; but the most valuable thing about knowledge is not where it takes a person, but how it changes the way one understands the world. Taylor's social imaginary, I think, fits well here. A society is governed not only by institutions but also by its self-imagining. How people understand the state, civilization, freedom, strength, and dignity will ultimately determine what realities they can accept.

It is precisely for this reason that intellectuals inherently carry a responsibility to transform society.

But by transforming society, I do not mean condescendingly educating others, nor forcibly imposing a theory onto reality. The more specific work is to clarify chaotic problems, to re-see people flattened by grand narratives, and to take seemingly self-evident judgments and ask again: Why does it hold? What does it obscure? Will it lead society toward openness, or toward a more refined closure?

So the responsibility of intellectuals is not self-justification. It is not to prove that one is more awake than the masses, not to prove that one's civilization is superior, and not to prove that one's path will ultimately prevail. The difficulty is, before wanting to transform society, to first reflect on what worldview one carries. If one carries civilizational centrism, a narrative of victory and defeat, and 19th-century power logic to transform society, then the so-called transformation is likely to create new closure.

## Youth leaders must first transform their own worldview

If the responsibility of intellectuals is to clarify issues, then youth leaders simply amplify that responsibility.

I don't really like understanding "youth leaders" as those who succeed earlier, become famous faster, or possess more resources. Those are just external positions. The issue is: when a young person, due to professional competence, historical positioning, and public influence, begins to affect more people, their judgments are no longer merely private. How they understand the world will influence how others understand technology, the nation, civilization, and themselves.

What youth leaders should be most wary of is not a lack of ability, but a lack of reflection. Insufficient ability only limits what one can do; insufficient reflection allows one to scale up a flawed worldview.

Civilizational centrism is especially dangerous here. A capable, ambitious, and zeitgeisty young person can easily bind their growth experience to national narratives: I succeed, so my path is correct; my nation rises, so my civilization is superior; my field leads, so my camp will inevitably win. I understand this passion and don't want to simply mock it. But it still hasn't moved beyond a 19th-century worldview: the world is an arena, civilizations are camps, technology is a weapon, and victory is the ultimate proof.

I think what is more worth pursuing is not this kind of victory.

China certainly needs to be strong, and Chinese youth certainly need to have their place on the world stage. But the responsibility at a higher level is not to replace Eurocentrism with Sinocentrism, but to participate in building a world that is no longer defined by a single center. Liu Qing's move from "tianxia" to "new cosmopolitanism"—I understand the key point here: not denying national interests and real competition, but after acknowledging differences, conflicts, and interests, still not reducing the world to zero-sum struggle.

## From Professional Competence to Public Responsibility

This becomes more concrete in the technological age.

AI, computational infrastructure, life sciences, energy systems, cybersecurity, automation, and data platforms are no longer mere professional tools. They are reshaping social relations and the world order. If a technologist truly advances to the forefront in a field, what they possess goes beyond personal capability; it is also a public force.

Merton spoke of universalism and organized skepticism in the scientific community; Ostrom spoke of commons needing governance. These all remind us of the same thing: knowledge is not the private property of any camp, nor is unconditional openness a panacea. An algorithm, a theory, a system should first be subject to evidence, logic, reproducibility, and peer criticism; papers, code, data, models, standards, and benchmarks should also enter a sustainable, monitorable, and participatory knowledge commons as much as possible.

So, when I think about the responsibility of youth leaders, I don't want to understand it as "representing China to win world recognition." That is still too narrow. I want to ask: Can I use my knowledge, technology, and judgment to help form a world that is more open, more discussable, and more accommodating of others? Can I, after gaining capability, not turn it into new arrogance, but into conditions for co-construction?

So, back to the initial question: What is the future of Chinese political philosophy?

My current tentative judgment is: China's most valuable intellectual contribution in the future is not to prove that Chinese civilization is superior to Western civilization, nor to prove that the Chinese model can replace the Western model, but to propose a less narrow worldview. It should avoid both Eurocentrism and Sinocentrism. It acknowledges national interests but does not sanctify them. It cherishes local traditions but is willing to let them be transformed by modern equality, rights, and public reason.

The strong Chinese youth I hope for should certainly be able to compete in the world, but should also have the ability to change the imagination of competition itself: less self-justification, more building common standards; recognizing others, rather than only worrying about whether they themselves are recognized; including technological consequences in their responsibilities, rather than treating technology merely as a tool for victory or defeat.

This may be the concrete meaning of what is today called "making a significant contribution to human civilization."
