In current AI and deep learning theory research, the term "manifold" is frequently used to describe data distributions, representation spaces, or constraint sets. This is not necessarily an exaggeration of theoretical depth; often it is merely borrowing geometric intuition or a convenient naming convention. However, without further specifying whether the "manifold" here is a strict mathematical object, a locally approximate model, or merely a set satisfying constraints, discussions can lose precision, and the methodological benefits remain quite limited.

## Methodology Issues

In interdisciplinary research, the fundamental motivation for introducing concepts from a specific mathematical branch is to invoke the mature mathematical tools and theorems established in that field, thereby deriving new conclusions. For example, when we place a specific machine learning problem within the framework of information theory, we can subsequently use information divergence or various information inequalities for derivation; when the problem is categorized as a statistics problem, concentration inequalities become core tools for analyzing generalization error bounds; or when the problem is abstracted as a convex optimization problem, researchers can leverage systematic convex analysis tools to prove the convergence and convergence rate of algorithms.

However, in current AI research, "manifold" is often used merely as a conceptual label. Many works are satisfied with claiming that "data lies on a low-dimensional manifold" or that "a certain class of constrained objects lies on a manifold," without further employing core tools from differential geometry, such as curvature, geodesics, local coordinate transformations, or integration and measure structures on manifolds. This does not mean these works lack value; it only means that "manifold" here often serves more as a modeling language than as a mathematical framework sufficient to derive new theorems. If a concept does not bring along its tool system, its explanatory power should be carefully evaluated.

## Local Properties and Global Structure

Tian Yuandong has criticized this phenomenon in an interview ](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/1982024166560518507). Taking the "pothole manifold hypothesis" for explaining adversarial examples as an example: without specifying the topological structure, regularity conditions, embedding, or computable geometric quantities of the manifold in question, the mathematical explanatory power of such a claim is limited.

In high-dimensional spaces, many complex data distributions can be loosely described as low-dimensional structures, locally smooth objects, or subsets embedded in high-dimensional spaces. But merely claiming "this is a manifold" without specifying its topological structure or geometric characteristics provides very limited information. Intrinsic dimension, local smoothness, or local approximability are mostly local properties and may not suffice to characterize global structures related to generalization, robustness, or optimization dynamics. The issue is not that the term "manifold" cannot be used, but that this naming alone cannot bear the explanatory burden.

## An Example

Here, we take DeepSeek's [《mHC: Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections》](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24880) as an example. The "manifold" in that paper is more of an engineering or geometric intuition naming, not necessarily corresponding to strict Riemannian manifold optimization.

If the core operation merely treats a set $\mathcal{S}$ satisfying specific constraints as a manifold $\mathcal{M}$ and performs constrained updates on it, then mathematically it resembles a constrained optimization problem. Calling such an object a manifold is not necessarily wrong; under appropriate regularity conditions, the constraint set may indeed form a manifold. However, if the paper does not further use Riemannian metrics, tangent space projections, geodesics, or other key structures from manifold optimization, then a more accurate statement would be that the authors adopted a geometric naming rather than providing a rigorous manifold optimization theory. What truly needs discussion is not whether the name is "advanced," but whether it corresponds to additional mathematical content.

## Conclusion

Scientific research should avoid letting naming bear theoretical commitments beyond its argumentative capacity. If the concept of "manifold" is used in AI theoretical analysis, one should clarify whether it is a strict differential geometry definition, a local approximation, a modeling intuition, or an engineering expression. If the research essentially deals with constrained optimization problems or low-dimensional structure assumptions, then using the corresponding language directly is usually more accurate than vaguely appealing to "manifold." The development of theoretical machine learning ultimately relies on testable definitions, clear assumptions, and solid mathematical derivations.
